# Central Sensitization Recovery Plan

---

## What's Actually Happening (Read This First)

Your nervous system has been in threat-detection mode since July — that's nine months. It has learned that your lower leg equals danger, and it has gotten so efficient at this that it is now generating signals in your face, your back, and potentially other places. This is called **central sensitization**: your central nervous system's threat threshold has dropped so low that almost any input — touch, movement, attention, even answering questions — can trigger a pain response.

To confirm your fact-check: yes, nerve pain and central sensitization can absolutely imitate bone stress pain AND create what feels like tenderness directly over the bone. This is allodynia — the sensitized nervous system generates pain at specific mapped locations when they are touched or pressed, and that pain feels identical to structural bone pain because your brain is producing the same signal. It is not the bone. This is part of why Corrado believed the poking session couldn't have physically done what it appeared to do.

The goal of this plan is to teach your nervous system that your body is safe. You do this through five mechanisms:
1. Giving the brain accurate information (no ongoing damage)
2. Normalizing sensory input in the affected area (desensitization)
3. Retraining the brain's body map (Graded Motor Imagery)
4. Reducing the baseline state of physiological threat (nervous system regulation)
5. Breaking the attention-pain feedback loop (behavioral rules)

## Daily Protocol

### Morning Block — 7 minutes (do this before leaving the house)

**Extended Exhale Breathing — 5 minutes**

Inhale through your nose for 4 counts. Exhale through your mouth for 6 to 8 counts. Repeat continuously for 5 minutes.

This is not relaxation fluff. The extended exhale directly activates the vagus nerve and shifts your autonomic nervous system toward parasympathetic state. You are starting your day by biologically telling your threat system to stand down before school stress compounds it.

**Intention Setting — 2 minutes**

Before you leave, say this once, out loud or in your head: *"I am not checking my leg today. If pain appears, I will notice it and move on."*

This is behavioral priming, not an affirmation. You are setting the rule before the situations that test it arise.

---

### Afternoon Block — 3:00 to 3:30pm (do this before gym or bike)

This is the most important block of the day. Do not skip it for the gym.

**1. Laterality Training — 8 minutes**

Download the app **"Recognise"** by NOI Group. There is a free version. Select the foot and ankle program.

The app shows you photographs of feet at different angles and orientations and you tap whether it is a left or right foot as fast as you can. It tracks your speed and accuracy.

- Session goal: work toward 80% accuracy or higher before progressing to the next phase
- Why this works: Sensitized brains consistently show reduced accuracy on left/right limb recognition tasks. This is because chronic pain physically distorts the brain's body schema — the internal map of your body. Retraining laterality recognition directly rewires the threat map associated with that limb.
- Important rule: If doing this exercise reliably triggers pain, stop for that day and try again tomorrow. Do not push through pain during this exercise.

**2. Sensory Desensitization — 7 minutes**

Materials needed: one soft cloth (an old t-shirt works), one slightly coarser cloth (a towel)

Method:
- Begin on your upper leg/thigh — stroke it slowly with the soft cloth for 1 minute
- Move to your knee area — 1 minute
- Move to your calf — 1 minute
- Move toward the lower leg and fibula area only if there is no significant pain response at the calf
- If there is a pain response, stay at the calf level and repeat tomorrow
- Never force the cloth directly onto the most sensitive area

The goal is progressive normalization of sensory processing by working from areas the brain maps as safe toward the areas it has flagged as dangerous. Over days and weeks, the boundary moves.

**3. Motor Imagery — 5 minutes**

Sit or lie down. Close your eyes. Vividly imagine yourself performing these movements in sequence, spending about 60 seconds on each:

- Walking normally across a grass field
- Doing a lateral shuffle, side to side
- Striking a ball cleanly with your left foot
- A short sprint
- Jumping and landing

You are not moving. Only your brain is. Vividness matters — try to feel the ground, hear the ball, feel the air. Dim, vague imagery is less effective. This reactivates dormant motor programs without triggering a pain response, and it begins to rebuild the movement maps your brain has been avoiding since July.

---

### Evening Block — before bed, 15 minutes

**Mirror Therapy — 7 minutes**

Setup: Sit in a chair. Place your full-length mirror vertically between your legs, angled so that it reflects your RIGHT (unaffected) leg. Cover or position your left leg so it is completely out of sight. Look only at the mirror.

Perform these movements with your right foot while watching the mirror:
- Slow foot circles, both directions
- Flexing and pointing the foot
- Heel raises
- Ankle tilts side to side

Your brain receives visual input of two normal legs moving symmetrically. This directly conflicts with the threat map and begins to override it. Mirror therapy has the strongest evidence base of any tool in this plan for central sensitization and CRPS.

- Start at 5 minutes for the first week. Work up to 10 to 12 minutes by the end of week 2.

**Wind-Down Breathing — 5 minutes**

Same protocol as the morning. Do this in bed. This is your system telling itself it survived the day and the threat is not active.

**Neutral Body Awareness — 3 minutes**

Lie flat, eyes closed. Move your awareness slowly through each body part for about 10 seconds each, in this order: feet, calves, knees, thighs, hips, stomach, chest, shoulders, arms, face.

You are practicing neutral attention — noticing each area without judging or hunting for pain. This is different from scanning. You are not checking whether something hurts. You are briefly visiting each area and leaving.

## Behavioral Rules — These Are Non-Negotiable

**Rule 1: No body scanning.**
You do not check your leg. You do not press on it with your fingers to see if it is tender. You do not lie in bed running through whether it hurts. Every time you scan, you are sending a threat signal to your brain and reinforcing the exact map you are trying to erase.

**Rule 2: The new spot rule.**
When pain appears in a new location — like your back did while you were answering questions — you respond with exactly this: *"That's my nervous system. It is not damage."* Then you deliberately redirect your attention to something external. You do not press on the new spot. You do not focus on it. You do not analyze it in your head. Acknowledge and redirect.

**Rule 3: Keep a brief daily pain log.**
Two minutes per day. Note the date, location(s) of pain, intensity out of 10, and what you were doing. This is data collection, not attention to pain. Keep it in this folder. It will show you the trend over weeks, and trends matter more than individual days.

**Rule 4: No pre-loading the MRI results.**
You will get the MRIs. Whatever they show, this plan continues. Even if there is residual bone stress present, the nervous system work still happens in parallel. These are not competing approaches.

## Non-Impact Conditioning — Keep Doing

**Bike HIIT:** Continue as is. This is one of the best things you can do — cardiovascular conditioning with zero ground reaction force, and the rhythmic, bilateral movement is actually settling for the nervous system.

**Swimming / Aqua jogging:** Reintroduce if not already happening. Hydrostatic pressure has a documented calming effect on the autonomic nervous system, and it is the closest thing to running mechanics without impact.

**Foam rolling:** Continue. Avoid rolling directly over the fibula itself. Everything else is fine.

## Education — Start This Week

Get **"Explain Pain"** by Lorimer Moseley and David Butler.

This is the clinical standard text for pain neuroscience education. Reading it is not optional background material — it is an active intervention. Research consistently shows that patients who receive thorough pain neuroscience education report measurable reductions in pain intensity. Your brain needs new information to update its threat assessment. Right now it is operating on old data that says your lower leg is in danger. This book gives it accurate data.

## Weekly Check-In (Track These Numbers)

Each week, note the following:
- Recognise app accuracy score (%)
- Mirror therapy: any changes in sensation or pain during it
- New body locations of pain that appeared (or didn't)
- General pain trend: better, same, or worse compared to last week
- Compliance: which sessions you missed and why

---

## Phase Progression

**Weeks 1–2:** Full protocol above. Priority is building the habit and starting laterality training from scratch.

**Weeks 3–4:** If laterality accuracy is at 80% or above, advance motor imagery to more complex and sport-specific movements — dribbling at speed, explosive cuts, shooting. Increase mirror therapy to 10 to 12 minutes. Begin desensitization directly over the fibula area if the surrounding tissue has normalized.

**Week 5 onward:** Introduce graded exposure to light impact only if MRI results support it and the pain trend is consistently downward. Return to Eric with impact-appropriate sessions.

---

## What to Watch For

**Good signs:**
- Laterality accuracy improving week over week
- Mirror therapy sessions with no pain response, or reduced pain response
- Pain staying in fewer locations rather than spreading
- The "new spot" redirecting strategy getting easier to execute

**Flag for Amelia or Corrado:**
- Pain spreading to entirely new regions of the body (upper body, opposite leg)
- The face/chills event repeating or intensifying
- Any symptom that feels autonomic — skin color changes, temperature changes in the limb, sweating differences between legs

The face and chills event is in that second category. If it happens again, document the exact circumstances — what you were doing, your stress level, whether you had been thinking about pain — and bring it to the pain specialists appointment. That detail is not nothing.
